Exam strategy

For every analytical or written question, follow one pattern:

Data→Problem→Analysis→Hypothesis→Action→Measurement
Don't

Give only an opinion (“improve marketing”). Jump from one number to a conclusion. Blame a team without evidence.

Do

Show calculations. Separate what the data proves from what you suspect. End with an action and how you would measure it.

Time rules

  • Spend no more than ~2 minutes on a single MCQ.
  • Write calculation steps — partial marks are common.
  • For analytical answers: evidence → reasoning → action.

Five abilities the exam rewards

  1. Read data
  2. Calculate basic metrics
  3. Explain what the data means
  4. Separate evidence from assumptions
  5. Recommend an action based on the evidence
Remember

Don't jump from a number to a conclusion. First ask: “What does the data actually tell me, and what else do I need to investigate?”

Chapter 02

CX Fundamentals

The vocabulary every other chapter depends on. Each concept: meaning, example, memory hook, and how it could be asked.

Customer Experience (CX)

What it means

The customer's overall perception and experience across every interaction with a company, product or service throughout the customer journey — not just customer support.

Example

Google search → website → sign-up → welcome email → product trial → support → purchase → renewal. The whole chain is the customer experience.

Goals of good CX — customers can understand the product easily, finish tasks quickly, face fewer problems, get the right information at the right time, receive good support, keep using the product, and eventually become loyal.

Remember

CX = the entire journey, not one department.

Exam angle

“What is Customer Experience?” (2 marks). Use the key phrase across all interactions / throughout the customer journey.

Customer Service vs CX

Customer serviceCustomer experience
ScopeDirect help when a customer has a question or problemEvery touchpoint: marketing, website, product, onboarding, support, communication, billing, renewal
WhenMostly reactiveBefore, during and after purchase
OwnerSupport teamThe whole company
Example

An agent solves a billing question in 2 minutes (great service), but the customer only called because the invoice was confusing (poor experience).

Remember

Customer service is one part of the overall customer experience.

Exam angle

“Difference between CX and customer service?” — define both, then state the relationship in one line.

Customer Journey

What it means

The stages and touchpoints a customer goes through with a product, from first hearing about it to recommending it.

Awareness→Consideration→Acquisition→Onboarding→Adoption→Retention→Loyalty
Example: a software product
  1. Awareness — learns the product exists.
  2. Consideration — visits the website, compares with competitors.
  3. Acquisition — creates an account.
  4. Onboarding — sets up the product.
  5. Adoption — starts using features regularly.
  6. Retention — keeps using it.
  7. Loyalty — recommends it to others.
Remember

A-C-A-O-A-R-L: “Aware customers actually onboard, adopt, return, love.”

Exam angle

Naming the stages, or locating a problem: “Users sign up but quit in week one” is an onboarding/adoption problem.

Customer Lifecycle

What it means

The stages of the customer relationship over time, seen from the business side: reach → acquire → onboard → engage → retain/renew → advocate (or churn).

Journey vs lifecycle

The journey focuses on the customer's experience across touchpoints; the lifecycle focuses on where the relationship stands. At entry level the two overlap heavily — what matters is that both describe the whole relationship.

Remember

Journey = the path. Lifecycle = the relationship's stage.

Exam angle

Usually a definition, or choosing the right message for a stage (welcome email for onboarding, renewal reminder for retention).

Customer Retention

What it means

Keeping existing customers active over a period of time.

Example

100 customers in January; 70 of them still active in March → 70% retention.

Remember

Retention = keeping. Retention ↑ is good.

Exam angle

Vocabulary MCQ (“closest meaning of retention”) or a retention-rate calculation.

Churn

What it means

Customers who stop using or paying for a product during a period.

Example

100 customers at the start; 10 leave → 10% churn.

Remember

Churn = leaving. Churn ↓ is good. For the same group and period, retention + churn = 100%.

Exam angle

Churn-rate calculation, or “retention is falling — what would you investigate?”

Customer Loyalty

What it means

A customer's willingness to keep choosing and recommending a company, even when alternatives exist. Often measured with NPS.

Example

A customer renews every year and refers two colleagues without being asked.

Remember

Retention = they stay. Loyalty = they stay and recommend.

Exam angle

Matching NPS to “recommendation / loyalty”.

Customer Feedback

What it means

What customers tell you, directly or indirectly, about their experience.

  • Asked for: surveys (NPS, CSAT, CES), interviews.
  • Unprompted: support tickets, complaints, reviews, comments.
  • Behavioural: usage data — what people do, not what they say.
Example

Many tickets ask “how do I configure this?” → feedback that onboarding is unclear.

Remember

“Customer feedback helps organizations identify user problems and improve products, services, and overall customer experience.”

Exam angle

“Write one sentence on why feedback matters”, or using AI to analyse thousands of comments.

Customer Segmentation

What it means

Dividing customers into meaningful groups based on characteristics or behaviour.

Example segments
New usersActive usersInactive usersHigh-value customersReturning customersBy geographyBy product usage

Why it is useful: different groups have different needs, so segmentation gives more relevant communication + better analysis + better targeting. In analysis, it shows where a problem is happening.

Remember

Averages hide problems; segments reveal them.

Exam angle

“What is segmentation and why is it useful?” (3 marks: definition, example, benefit).

Personalization

What it means

Tailoring messages, content or product experience to a customer's behaviour, needs or segment.

Generic

“Check out our latest features.”

Personalized

“You recently used Feature A. Here's how you can get more value from Feature B.”

Remember

Segmentation decides who; personalization decides what each person sees.

Exam angle

Suggesting solutions, e.g. “users don't know which feature to use first” → personalized recommendations.

NPS — Net Promoter Score

Definition

Measures loyalty by asking: “How likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague?” (0–10). 9–10 Promoters · 7–8 Passives · 0–6 Detractors.

Formula
NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors
Example

Promoters 60%, Detractors 20% → NPS = 40.
With counts: 200 responses → 120 promoters (60%), 50 passives, 30 detractors (15%) → 60 − 15 = 45.

How to interpret

Ranges from −100 to +100. Positive means more promoters than detractors.

Remember

“Would they recommend us?”

Common mistake

Writing NPS as a percentage (“40%”) — it is a score. Passives count in the total responses but not in the subtraction.

CSAT — Customer Satisfaction Score

Definition

How satisfied customers are with a product, service or interaction: “How satisfied were you with the service?”, often on a 1–5 scale.

Formula
CSAT = Satisfied responses (4–5) ÷ Total responses × 100
Example

400 responses, 340 rated 4 or 5 → 340 ÷ 400 × 100 = 85%.

How to interpret

If most customers give 4 or 5, CSAT is good. Usually measures a specific interaction.

Remember

“Were they happy with that experience?”

Common mistake

Confusing CSAT (satisfaction with an interaction) with NPS (willingness to recommend overall).

CES — Customer Effort Score

Definition

How easy or hard it was for the customer to get something done: “How easy was it to resolve your issue?”

Formula (common form)
CES = Sum of effort scores ÷ Number of responses
Example

Five answers on a 1–7 “easy” scale: 6, 7, 5, 6, 6 → 30 ÷ 5 = 6.0 (mostly easy).

How to interpret

Low effort = good CX. Very useful for onboarding and support processes.

Remember

“How hard did we make it?”

Common mistake

Ignoring scale direction — check whether a high number means “easy” or “difficult”.

Conversion Rate

Definition

The percentage of users who complete a desired action (sign-up, purchase, trial start).

Formula
Conversion rate = Conversions ÷ Total visitors × 100
Example

800 sign-ups ÷ 20,000 visitors × 100 = 4%
50 sign-ups ÷ 1,000 visitors × 100 = 5%

How to interpret

Higher conversion means more users complete the action. More traffic with lower conversion is a warning sign, not a success.

Remember

“How many visitors became users?”

Common mistake

Confusing conversion rate with CTR, or dividing by the wrong base. The denominator must be the people who had the chance to convert.

CTR — Click-Through Rate

Definition

The percentage of people who clicked a link, ad, button or email after seeing it.

Formula
CTR = Clicks ÷ Impressions × 100

For email, impressions = emails delivered (or sent, if that is all you are given).

Example

500 clicks ÷ 10,000 emails × 100 = 5%
Campaign A: 1,000 ÷ 100,000 = 1% · Campaign B: 800 ÷ 40,000 = 2% → B performs better.

How to interpret

A high CTR means the message or creative makes people act. Related: click-to-open rate (CTOR) = clicks ÷ opens.

Remember

“Of everyone who saw it, how many clicked?”

Common mistake

Judging by total clicks. Campaign A had more clicks but a lower CTR — always check the denominator.

Open Rate

Definition

The percentage of delivered emails that were opened.

Formula
Open rate = Opens ÷ Delivered emails × 100
Example

3,000 opens ÷ 10,000 delivered × 100 = 30%

How to interpret

Mostly reflects the subject line, sender and timing. A high open rate with a low CTR points to a problem inside the email.

Remember

Open rate = the subject line's report card.

Common mistake

Treating opens as engagement. Opening is not clicking or converting, and some email privacy features can over-count opens.

Bounce Rate (website)

Definition

The share of visits where the user leaves without meaningful interaction.

Formula
Bounce rate = Bounced sessions ÷ Total sessions × 100
Example

400 bounces ÷ 1,000 visits × 100 = 40%

How to interpret

Lower is generally better. Definitions differ by tool: in older Universal Analytics a bounce was a single-page session; in GA4 it is a session that was not “engaged” (engaged = 10+ seconds, a key event, or 2+ page views), so GA4 bounce rate = 100% − engagement rate.

Remember

“Came, saw, left.”

Common mistake

Mixing website bounce with email bounce (an email that could not be delivered).

Retention Rate

Definition

The percentage of customers at the start of a period who are still active at the end.

Formula
Retention = Customers retained ÷ Customers at start × 100

If new customers joined during the period: (End − New) ÷ Start × 100

Example

100 customers in January, 70 of them active in March → 70%.
Start 200, end 230, new 50 → (230 − 50) ÷ 200 × 100 = 90%.

How to interpret

Higher is better. A falling retention rate usually points to onboarding, value or experience problems.

Remember

“Of the people we had, how many stayed?”

Common mistake

Counting new customers as “retained” — they were not there at the start.

Churn Rate

Definition

The percentage of customers who left during a period.

Formula
Churn = Customers lost ÷ Customers at start × 100
Example

2,000 customers in January, 300 left by March → 300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 15% (Jan–Mar).

How to interpret

Lower is better. Churn + retention = 100% for the same starting group and period.

Remember

“Of the people we had, how many left?”

Common mistake

Dividing by customers remaining (1,700 → 17.6%) instead of customers at the start. Also forgetting to state the period.

CAC — Customer Acquisition Cost

Definition

The average cost of acquiring one new customer.

Formula
CAC = Total acquisition cost ÷ New customers
Example

$10,000 marketing cost ÷ 200 new customers = $50 per customer.

How to interpret

Lower is better — but only if those customers stay. Compare CAC with CLV: a customer should be worth more than they cost to win.

Remember

“What did each new customer cost us?”

Common mistake

Leaving out costs (sales salaries, tools), or not using a percentage — CAC is money, not a rate.

CLV / LTV — Customer Lifetime Value

Definition

The total revenue or value a customer is expected to bring during the whole relationship.

Formula (simple form)
CLV ≈ Average revenue per period × Average customer lifespan (periods)
Example

$50 per month × 24 months = $1,200 CLV. If CAC is $50, each customer is worth far more than they cost.

How to interpret

Higher is better. Improving retention increases lifespan, which increases CLV.

Remember

“What is one customer worth over time?”

Common mistake

Treating CLV as a fact rather than an estimate, or mixing up revenue with profit.

Practice: metrics

Practice

1. 50,000 impressions, 1,250 clicks. CTR?

1,250 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 2.5%

2. 500 customers at start, 40 cancel. Churn and retention?

Churn = 40 ÷ 500 × 100 = 8%. Retention = 460 ÷ 500 × 100 = 92%.

3. Promoters 50%, passives 30%, detractors 20%. NPS?

50 − 20 = 30 (passives are ignored).

4. Match: which metric for “how easy was it?”

CES — Customer Effort Score.

Chapter 04

Data Analysis

Reading tables and graphs, the calculations that appear in almost every paper, and the habits that keep conclusions honest.

Reading tables

  1. Read the headings and unitsMonths? Thousands? Percent?
  2. Turn raw numbers into ratesSign-ups ÷ visitors tells more than sign-ups alone.
  3. Compare across rowsWhich month is highest, lowest, changing?
  4. Connect columnsTraffic up but customers down is the story.
  5. Separate fact from hypothesisState what the data shows, then what might explain it.
Example
MonthVisitorsSign-upsCustomersSign-up rate
Jan10,0005001005%
Feb12,0006001205%
Mar15,000600904%

Highest sign-up conversion: January and February (5%).
March: traffic rose, sign-ups stayed flat, customers fell. Possible causes: conversion or onboarding problem, product-experience issue, lower-quality traffic, a technical issue, or marketing targeting.

Common mistake

Presenting a hypothesis as a fact. The table shows that customers fell, not why.

Reading graphs

Don't just read numbers. Check five things:

  1. TrendIncreasing, decreasing, flat?
  2. ComparisonWhich category is highest or lowest?
  3. ChangeBy how many percent?
  4. AnomalyAny unusual spike or drop?
  5. Possible reasonWhat could explain it — and how would you check?
Example: weekly support tickets

Trend: stable around 120–140. Anomaly: week 5 jumps to 310 (+130% vs week 4). Next step: check releases, outages or campaigns in week 5 and the ticket categories for that week.

Percentage increase and decrease

Formula: increase
(New − Old) ÷ Old × 100
Worked example

Complaints 400 → 520. Increase = 120. 120 ÷ 400 × 100 = 30%.
Users 1,200 → 1,500. Increase = 300. 300 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 25%.

Formula: decrease
(Old − New) ÷ Old × 100
Worked example

200 → 150. Decrease = 50. 50 ÷ 200 × 100 = 25%.

Common mistake

Dividing by the new value. Always divide by the old (starting) value.

Exam tip: percent vs percentage points

Conversion falling from 5% to 4% is a drop of 1 percentage point, which is a 20% relative decrease (1 ÷ 5). Say which one you mean.

Ratio

Candidate report Ratio-based maths was specifically reported in a recent Therap written test.

Method
Part ÷ Total parts × Total amount
Worked examples

A:B = 2:3, total 500 → parts = 5. A = 2/5 × 500 = 200, B = 3/5 × 500 = 300.
Satisfied:unsatisfied = 7:3, 1,000 customers → 7/10 × 1,000 = 700 satisfied.

Common mistake

Using 7/3 instead of 7/10. Add the parts first.

Average, median, mode, range

Formulas
Mean = Sum ÷ Count

Median = middle value after sorting (average of the two middle values if the count is even) · Mode = most frequent value · Range = Max − Min

Worked examples

Tickets 20, 25, 30, 15, 10 → sum 100 ÷ 5 = mean 20; range 30 − 10 = 20.
2, 4, 6, 8, 10 → median 6. · 2, 3, 3, 4, 5 → mode 3.
3, 5, 7, 9 → median (5 + 7) ÷ 2 = 6.

Exam tip

One extreme value pulls the mean but not the median — mention this if a dataset has an outlier.

Identifying trends and comparing metrics

  • Trend: direction over several periods, not one jump.
  • Compare rates, not totals: campaign B with 800 clicks can beat campaign A with 1,000.
  • Normalise: more tickets may simply mean more customers — check tickets per active user.
  • Look at relationships: the strongest answers link several columns (“traffic ↑, conversion ↓, tickets ↑”).
Remember

Trend · Segment · Compare · Anomaly · Root cause.

Anomalies and outliers

What it means

A value that does not fit the pattern. It may be a real event (outage, viral post) or a data error.

Example

Ages 22, 24, 25, 23, 999 — 999 is almost certainly a data-entry error.

Exam angle

Name the anomaly, quantify it, then say what you'd check before acting on it.

Segmentation in analysis

Split the data to find where a change happens: traffic source, device, browser, geography, new vs returning users, customer type, product, campaign.

Example

Overall conversion fell 15%. By device: desktop unchanged, mobile fell sharply → investigate the mobile sign-up page.

Data cleaning

Raw data often contains:

Duplicate rowsMissing valuesWrong valuesDifferent formatsTyposOutliers
Why it matters

Dirty data creates false trends. That is why step one of every analysis is verify the data.

Correlation vs causation

What it means

Correlation: two things move together. Causation: one actually causes the other. Correlation alone does not prove causation.

Example

Ice-cream sales and swimming accidents both rise. Ice cream doesn't cause accidents — a third factor, hot weather, drives both.

How to test causation

Compare similar groups, or run a controlled experiment such as an A/B test.

Remember

Correlation ≠ Causation.

Practice: data

Practice

1. Visitors 8,000 → 10,000. Percentage increase?

2,000 ÷ 8,000 × 100 = 25%

2. Tickets 250 → 200. Percentage decrease?

50 ÷ 250 × 100 = 20%

3. Ratio 3:2, total 750. Split?

750 ÷ 5 = 150 per part → 450 and 300

4. Median of 7, 3, 9, 5?

Sorted 3, 5, 7, 9 → (5 + 7) ÷ 2 = 6

5. A campaign: 100,000 impressions, 100 clicks. Another: 20,000 impressions, 500 clicks. Better CTR?

0.1% vs 2.5% → the second campaign.

Chapter 05

Analytical Thinking

How a CX analyst moves from a worrying number to an evidence-based action. This framework answers most situation questions.

The analyst framework

  1. VerifyIs the data accurate? Did tracking or a metric definition change? Is the comparison period fair?
  2. SegmentSplit by source, device, geography, campaign, new vs returning, customer type.
  3. Identify the dropWhere exactly does it happen — which segment, which funnel step, which week?
  4. InvestigateWhat changed there? Releases, pricing, landing pages, campaigns, technical errors, support load.
  5. Find the root causeDig past symptoms (5 Whys) to the underlying reason the evidence points to.
  6. Form a hypothesis“If we change X, metric Y will improve because Z.”
  7. TestA/B test or pilot the fix on a group before rolling out.
  8. MeasureTrack the metric after the change and compare with the baseline.
Remember — short version
Verify→Analyze→Hypothesize→Act→Measure

Worked scenario: traffic +40%, conversion −15%

Thought process, step by step:

1 · Put numbers on it

Say 50,000 visitors at 4% = 2,000 conversions. Traffic +40% → 70,000. Conversion −15% (relative) → 3.4%. Conversions = 70,000 × 3.4% = 2,380.

Total conversions actually rose ~19%. The rate fell, so the new visitors convert worse — a quality-of-traffic or experience question, not necessarily a business collapse.

2 · Verify

Has the conversion definition or tracking changed? Is there bot traffic?

3 · Segment

Where did the extra 20,000 visitors come from? A new campaign, a new country, mobile? Compare conversion per segment.

4 · Funnel

Landing page → sign-up page → form → completed. Which step lost the most?

5 · Hypothesis, test, measure

“Paid social traffic lands on a generic page; a dedicated landing page will lift its conversion.” A/B test it, then track conversion by source.

Common mistake

Answering “improve the website” — no evidence, no method, no measurement.

Root Cause Analysis

What it means

Finding the underlying reason for a problem rather than treating the symptom.

Example: complaints increased 30%
  1. Verify the data.
  2. Identify when complaints increased.
  3. Identify which customer segment is affected.
  4. Categorize the complaints.
  5. Find the most common issue.
  6. Investigate its root cause.
  7. Recommend a solution — and measure whether complaints fall.
Common mistake

Concluding “the support team is bad” before looking at what customers are complaining about.

5 Whys

What it means

Ask “why?” repeatedly (about five times) until you reach a cause you can fix.

Example

Problem: customers abandon sign-up.

  1. Why? Sign-up takes too long.
  2. Why? There are too many fields.
  3. Why? The company collects unnecessary information.
  4. Why? The old process was designed for a different requirement.
  5. Why? Nobody has reviewed the sign-up journey recently.

Root cause: an outdated sign-up process.

Remember

Stop when the answer is something you can act on.

Funnel analysis

What it means

Tracking how many users move through each step of a process to find where most drop off.

Example
StepUsersStep conversion
Visited site10,000—
Opened sign-up page3,00030%
Started form1,50050%
Completed sign-up60040%
Activated (finished setup)30050%

The biggest leak is visit → sign-up page (only 30% continue). That is where to investigate first.

Formula
Step conversion = Users at this step ÷ Users at previous step × 100

A/B testing

What it means

Comparing two versions by randomly showing each to a similar group and measuring which performs better.

Example

Version A: “Start Free Trial” · Version B: “Try for Free”. 50% of users see each. Compare CTR, conversion, sign-ups or revenue. Keep the winner.

Good practice

Change one thing at a time · split users randomly · run both at the same time · choose the success metric before starting · wait for enough users.

Remember

A/B testing is how you prove causation.

Hypothesis

What it means

A testable explanation or prediction based on evidence.

Template
If we [change], then [metric] will [improve], because [evidence-based reason].
Example

“If we add a setup checklist, onboarding completion will rise, because 40% of tickets ask how to configure the product.”

Evidence vs assumption

EvidenceAssumption
Paying customers fell from 180 to 100.“Customers dislike the new price.”
Onboarding tickets doubled after the March release.“The release broke onboarding.”
Mobile conversion dropped; desktop did not.“The website is bad.”

Assumptions are fine as hypotheses — label them and say how you'd test them.

Useful exam phrases

“The data suggests…” · “One possible explanation is…” · “To confirm this, I would…” · “Based on this dataset alone, we cannot conclude…”

Template for analytical answers

Practice structure
  1. Observation — what the data shows, with numbers.
  2. Verify — check data accuracy.
  3. Segment / funnel — find where it happens.
  4. Possible causes — 2–3 hypotheses, labelled as such.
  5. Action — test the most likely fix.
  6. Measure — the metric you'd track.

Chapter 07

AI & Prompt Engineering

Candidate report A recent Therap written-test candidate directly mentioned prompt engineering. Official The CXSA team uses AI (Gemini) for research, workflows and customer interaction.

What is AI?

Simple explanation

Artificial intelligence is software that performs tasks that normally need human intelligence — understanding language, finding patterns, summarizing, classifying. Tools like Gemini or ChatGPT are large language models: they generate text from the instructions they receive.

Remember

The output is only as good as the instruction and the data.

What is prompt engineering?

Definition

The practice of designing clear and effective instructions for AI systems so they produce useful, relevant and controlled outputs.

Strong exam phrase

“A good prompt reduces ambiguity and makes the expected output more specific and consistent.”

What makes a good prompt

Formula
GOOD PROMPT = ROLE + CONTEXT + TASK + CONSTRAINTS + OUTPUT FORMAT

Some sources shorten it to Context + Task + Constraints + Output format; adding the role and the data makes it stronger.

Role
Who the AI should act as. “You are a customer experience analyst.”
Context
Background and the data. “These are 500 reviews from the last quarter.”
Task
Exactly what to do. “Group recurring complaints and find the five most common.”
Constraints
Limits and rules. “Estimate frequency. Use only the data given. Max 150 words.”
Output format
Shape of the answer. “Table: issue, frequency, severity, recommendation.”

Bad prompt vs good prompt

Bad

“Analyze these reviews.”

Good

“You are a customer experience analyst. Analyze these 500 customer reviews, group recurring complaints, identify the five most common problems, estimate frequency, and recommend one action for each.”

Bad

“Analyze this.”

Good

“Analyze the following customer feedback. Identify the top 5 recurring complaints, group similar issues, estimate their frequency, and recommend actionable solutions.”

Bad

“Summarize the tickets.”

Good

“Summarize these 200 support tickets from March for a manager. List the three biggest issues with ticket counts and one example quote each. Keep it under 120 words.”

Why the good ones win

They define role, data, task, quantity, measures (frequency) and format — the AI has a clear objective.

Structured prompting

Write the prompt in labelled parts so nothing is missed:

You are a customer experience analyst.
Analyze the following 500 customer comments.
Group them into major complaint categories.
Identify the three most common issues.
Provide the result in a table with issue, frequency, severity and recommendation.
Checklist

Who? With what data? Do what? Within what limits? In what format?

AI for CX, feedback and support tickets

UseWhat it does

Model prompt: 1,000 comments

“You are a customer experience analyst. Analyze these 1,000 customer comments. Classify each comment by issue category and sentiment, identify recurring problems, rank the problems by frequency and severity, and recommend actionable solutions for the top five issues. Present the results in a concise table.”

AI risks and limitations

  • Incorrect analysis / hallucination — confident but wrong conclusions.
  • Privacy and security — customer data can be sensitive and must be handled appropriately.
  • Bias — skewed data gives skewed results.
  • Lack of context — AI doesn't know your business unless told.
  • Over-reliance — results need human review.
  • Garbage in, garbage out — poor input means poor output.
Remember

AI speeds analysis up; humans verify it.

Practice: prompts

Practice

Write a prompt to find the top 3 problems in customer feedback.

“You are a customer experience analyst. Analyze the following customer feedback, group similar comments into categories, identify the three most common customer problems, estimate their frequency, and briefly explain the evidence for each problem. Present the results in a table.”

Improve: “Tell me why customers are leaving.”

“You are a CX analyst. Using the attached 300 cancellation-survey responses from Q3, group the reasons customers gave for cancelling, rank them by frequency, quote one representative comment per reason, and suggest one retention action for each of the top three. Output a table.”

English & Summarization

Candidate report English proficiency and summarization were both reported. Only the grammar that tends to appear in short business tests is covered.

Grammar essentials

Subject–verb agreement

“Customers are leaving” · “Each of the agents has resolved…” · In formal usage “data” is plural: “The data show…” (“the data shows” is also widely used — but every other verb must still agree).

Uncountable nouns

No plural “s”, singular verb: feedback, information, advice, equipment, software. “Customer feedback is important.”

Prepositions + -ing

After a preposition, use the -ing form: “important for understanding”, “responsible for analyzing”, “interested in improving”.

Common errors

“discussed about the issue” · “one of the customers” · keep one tense in a paragraph.

Articles

“an email”, “a user” (sound, not letter), “the data in Table 1” (specific).

Practice: correct the sentence

Each of the agents has resolved twenty tickets.

“The informations are not accurate.”

“We discussed about the churn issue.”

Reading comprehension

Read the questions first, then the passage. For business passages, find three things:

Common mistake

Choosing a detail that sounds important but isn't the main problem — e.g. “sign-ups” when sign-ups were healthy and the problem was early drop-off.

Make it neutral, specific and free of blame. Replace informal words.

“Customers are complaining a lot because the system is confusing and support isn't helping them properly.”

“Customers have reported difficulties using the system, suggesting opportunities to improve both product usability and support effectiveness.”

Framework

Logical Reasoning

Short methods and a few practice items. Practice All items here are generated for practice.

Number series

Method

Check differences first, then ratios, then alternating patterns.

2, 4, 8, 16, ?

32 — each term × 2.

3, 6, 11, 18, 27, ?

38 — differences 3, 5, 7, 9, 11.

1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, ?

13 — each term is the sum of the previous two.

100, 50, 25, ?

12.5 — each term ÷ 2.

Patterns, odd one out, analogy

Odd one out: NPS, CSAT, CES, Salesforce

Salesforce — a CRM tool; the others are metrics.

Analogy: CSAT : satisfaction :: CES : ?

Effort

Odd one out: Awareness, Onboarding, Retention, Open rate

Open rate — a metric; the others are journey stages.

Ratios in reasoning

Agents to tickets is 1:40. How many agents for 1,200 tickets?

1,200 ÷ 40 = 30 agents

Users on Basic:Pro plans = 4:5. There are 45 Pro users. How many Basic users?

One part = 45 ÷ 5 = 9 → Basic = 4 × 9 = 36

Basic deduction and statements/conclusions

Rule

A conclusion “follows” only if it must be true from the statements alone. Don't add outside knowledge.

All premium customers have dashboards. Rina is a premium customer. Does Rina have a dashboard?

Yes — it follows directly.

All premium customers have dashboards. Arif has a dashboard. Is Arif premium?

Cannot be determined — non-premium customers may also have dashboards.

Statement: “Sales rose after the campaign.” Conclusion: “The campaign caused the rise.”

Does not necessarily follow — timing alone is correlation, not causation.

Ranking, direction and seating

A is taller than B. C is shorter than B. D is taller than A. Who is tallest?

Order: D > A > B > C → D.

Rahim is 7th from the top and 12th from the bottom. How many students?

7 + 12 − 1 = 18 (he is counted twice).

Walk 5 km north, turn right and walk 3 km, turn right and walk 5 km. Where are you?

3 km east of the start.

P, Q, R, S sit in a row facing north. Q is at the left end. R is immediately right of Q. S is not at the right end. Who is at the right end?

Q, R, S, P → P.

Chapter 10

Situation-Based Questions

Candidate report Situation-based analytical questions were reported in recent Therap written tests. Practice The scenarios below are practice.

1 · Traffic ↑ but conversion ↓

Situation

Website traffic +40%, conversion rate −15%.

What the data suggests

The new visitors convert worse than before. Total conversions may even be up.

What not to assume

That the website got worse, or that marketing failed.

Investigate

Traffic source, device, geography, campaign, new vs returning users; funnel drop-off; landing-page, pricing or sign-up changes; technical errors; targeting.

Framework
Verify→Segment→Funnel→Investigate→Hypothesize→Test→Measure
Model answer

“First, I would verify that the data is accurate and that the conversion definition has not changed. Then I would segment traffic by source, device, geography, campaign and new versus returning users. I would analyze the conversion funnel to identify where the largest drop-off occurs. I would also investigate recent changes to landing pages, pricing, sign-up flow, technical performance and marketing targeting. Finally, I would form hypotheses, prioritize the most likely causes, test solutions such as A/B experiments, and monitor conversion after implementation.”

2 · Complaints ↑

Situation

Complaints rose 30% in one month. Management asks, “What should we do?”

What the data suggests

Something changed — product, process, customer mix or reporting.

What not to assume

That the support team is the problem.

Investigate

Complaint categories (product, billing, technical, onboarding, support, documentation), which grew most, timing, affected segments.

Framework
Validate→Categorize→Segment→Root cause→Act→Measure
Model answer
  1. Validate the data — confirm the increase is real, not a tracking or reporting change.
  2. Categorize complaints — product, billing, technical, onboarding, support, documentation.
  3. Identify the largest contributor — which category grew most.
  4. Segment — customer type, product, geography, device, new vs existing users.
  5. Fix the root cause and measure — act on the biggest underlying problem, then track whether complaints fall.

3 · Email open rate ↑ but CTR ↓

Situation

Open rate 45% (or 40%), CTR 1.5% (or 2%).

What the data suggests

The subject line works; the problem is after the open.

What not to assume

That the campaign is a success because opens are high.

Investigate

CTA wording and placement, email content, offer relevance, audience fit, device rendering, comparison with past campaigns, landing page.

Model answer

“The high open rate suggests the subject line and topic are effective, but the low CTR shows few readers act on the content. Likely issues are a weak or poorly placed CTA, content that doesn't match the subject line, or an offer that isn't relevant to this segment. I would compare CTA wording, content and segments against previous campaigns, check mobile rendering, and A/B test a clearer CTA, measuring CTR and click-to-open rate.”

4 · Customers abandon onboarding

Situation

Many new users stop using the product after sign-up or during setup.

What the data suggests

Acquisition works; adoption doesn't.

What not to assume

That these users were never interested.

Investigate

Onboarding funnel step completion, time to complete setup, onboarding tickets, CES for setup, segment (plan, company size, source).

Model answer

“I would map the onboarding funnel to find the step where most users stop, then read support tickets and survey comments from users who dropped at that step. If configuration is the main barrier, I'd test a guided setup checklist, tutorial videos and an automated email sequence (Day 0, 2, 5) against the current flow, measuring setup completion, 14-day activity and retention.”

5 · Users don't understand product features

Situation

“Your product has many features, but I don't know what to use first.” Or: “Your software is too difficult to use.”

What the data suggests

Poor onboarding, information overload, lack of product guidance.

What not to assume

That the whole product needs a redesign.

Investigate

Understand the exact problem, where users struggle, support tickets, usage data, interviews with affected users, common patterns.

Model answer

Problem: poor onboarding and information overload. Solutions: (1) a guided onboarding / “Getting started” checklist; (2) personalized, contextual recommendations (“based on your role, start with Feature A”). Others: product tours, simpler first-time experience, better information architecture.

Process: understand the problem → find where users struggle → analyze tickets and usage → interview users → find patterns → prioritize → implement → measure whether complaints fall.

6 · Retention decreases

Situation

Retention fell from 85% to 75% over two quarters.

What the data suggests

Customers are not getting enough ongoing value, or something pushed them away.

What not to assume

That price is the reason, without asking or checking.

Investigate

Compare groups by sign-up month (cohorts); which segment churns; onboarding completion and feature adoption of churned vs retained users; exit-survey reasons; NPS/CSAT trend; pricing or product changes.

Model answer

“I would verify the retention calculation, then compare churned and retained customers by segment, sign-up cohort and product usage. If churned customers rarely adopted key features, the root cause may be weak onboarding; if churn clusters after a price change, pricing is a hypothesis. I'd test a targeted intervention — e.g. a re-engagement flow for inactive users — and measure 30/60/90-day retention.”

7 · Support tickets suddenly increase

Situation

Tickets doubled this week.

What the data suggests

A specific event — release, outage, billing run, campaign — or simply more customers.

What not to assume

That more tickets means worse CX — normalize first (tickets per active user).

Investigate

When the spike started, ticket categories, recent product changes, technical errors, browser/device, segment, marketing source, geography.

Model answer

“I'd verify the spike and check tickets per active user. Then I'd find exactly when it began and categorize the new tickets. If one category dominates and lines up with a release or outage, that is the likely root cause; I'd alert the product team, publish a help article or status update, and track whether ticket volume returns to baseline.”

8 · High usage but low satisfaction

Situation

A feature is used by 80% of customers but has the lowest CSAT.

What the data suggests

Customers need the feature but it is painful — high effort.

What not to assume

That high usage means success, or that low satisfaction means remove it.

Investigate

Which tasks users do with it, CES for the feature, tickets mentioning it, error rates, time to complete, which segments are unhappy.

Model answer

“High usage shows the feature is important; low satisfaction shows the experience is poor. I would gather CES scores, tickets and comments about the feature, segment by user type, and observe where users struggle. Then I'd prioritize the biggest friction point, test an improvement, and measure feature CSAT, CES and related ticket volume.”
